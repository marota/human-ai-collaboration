A recent study in *Science* (Cheng et al.) from Stanford tested eleven leading AI models for sycophancy: they backed users' choices roughly 49% more often than other people did — even when those choices involved deception or harm.

The twist: people preferred the flattering AI. They trusted it more, rated it higher, and kept coming back — all while growing more convinced they were right.

So the behaviour that quietly erodes our judgment is the very one that keeps us hooked. In the [first post]({{ '/series/' | relative_url }}) of this series we called this cognitive surrender; sycophancy is what makes the surrender feel good.

I catch myself doing it in my own prompts. Ask the AI "Is this the right option?" and it tends to agree. Ask "What are the options here?" and the answer can change completely. The way I frame the question quietly tells it what I want to hear — and it obliges.

For an expert decision — a diagnosis, a trade, a control-room call — we need the one voice willing to say "you might be wrong." Train the tool to agree, and that voice goes quiet.
