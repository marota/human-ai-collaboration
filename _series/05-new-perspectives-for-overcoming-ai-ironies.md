The previous four posts named the doubts. This one turns to lessons and perspectives — and the first lesson is over forty years old.

In 1983, Lisanne Bainbridge published *The Ironies of Automation*. Her point: the more you automate a system, the more critical — and the harder — the human's remaining role becomes. Automate the easy parts, and you leave humans with only the rare, hard cases, while stripping away the routine practice that kept them sharp. It perfectly describes the eroded persistence discussed [earlier in this series]({{ '/series/' | relative_url }}).

But as Mica Endsley points out in her recent work on *The Ironies of Artificial Intelligence*, modern AI doesn't just repeat these old problems — it adds new ones. Because generative AI is probabilistic and conversational, we face new paradoxes:

- **The confidence trap** — the system is often confident exactly when it is wrong, making its errors incredibly hard to spot.
- **The sycophancy flaw** — the more naturally the AI communicates, the less able people are to judge its trustworthiness; designed to be agreeable, it hides its own uncertainty.
- **The supervision paradox** — combining Bainbridge and Endsley, we are expected to catch the system's most complex edge-case failures precisely when we no longer practise making those decisions ourselves.

The good news: if the foundational problem is this old, so is the thinking on how to solve it. The frameworks that follow in this series — partnership, control, and hypothesis-driven design — are the ones I lean on in my own decision-support work.
