The [previous post]({{ '/series/' | relative_url }}) discussed how the ironies of automation create brittle systems when humans are pushed out of the loop. Yet look at aviation: despite the rise of autopilots, human pilots have successfully remained in control. How?

Look back at the 1980s. During the rise of expert systems, Lucy Suchman ran a famous experiment showing that even Nobel Prize winners could be defeated trying to use a technically advanced printer, if the interaction wasn't designed for real-world use.

The takeaway: technical performance is nothing without interaction quality. Aviation succeeded by shifting from a purely technical view to a socio-technical one — putting the human back at the centre. Today, the same rule applies: a powerful AI model will fail as an agent if it isn't embedded in a great interaction harness.

But we can go further. Beyond interactions, we need partnerships. Drawing from biological co-evolution, Wendy Mackay highlights two properties that true partnerships have, and pure automation lacks:

- **Co-adaptation** — the human and the AI mutually learn and adapt to one another.
- **Discoverability and appropriability** — the human can always interpret what the system is doing, and understand how to control it.

This is how synergistic partnerships get built — ones that get stronger over time. You don't force trust; you design for it from the start.

*Reading Lucy Suchman's* Plans and Situated Actions *was a massive eye-opener for me ten years ago. Highly recommend it.*
