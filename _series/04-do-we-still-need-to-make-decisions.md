The title of Éric Hazan and Olivier Sibony's book, *Faut-il encore décider ?* (*Do we still need to decide?*), is a deliberate provocation. The authors aren't asking whether human decision-making is still possible — they're asking whether it is still necessary.

When data is abundant and objectives are crystal clear, AI dominates. It produces highly effective decisions, making human intervention feel unnecessary.

But there is a catch: decisions are not just about efficiency. They are about shared acceptance. Making a decision is an act of commitment. It requires responsibility, authority, and moral judgment.

We also face many situations where data is scarce or objectives are ambiguous. We cannot delegate these complex choices to a system that cannot carry the weight of their consequences. As discussed in the [first post]({{ '/series/' | relative_url }}) of this series, we must resist cognitive surrender and keep training our own persistence for making decisions.

Yet AI isn't just a crutch. By mirroring our own limitations back at us, co-deciding with AI gives us a powerful tool to overcome our biases and improve how we operate.

In my domain of critical systems, human decision-making remains completely irreducible when it comes to safety. But as system complexity grows, AI assistance is no longer just a nice-to-have — it is becoming essential.
