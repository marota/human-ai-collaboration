The [previous post]({{ '/series/10-human-ai-needs-a-mission-control/' | relative_url }}) asked who should be in Europe's Mission Control for human–AI collaboration. Before naming the crews, a word on the disciplines they will need.

Most frameworks tell you what a trustworthy AI system must be. Few tell you who makes it so — who designs its autonomy, and who manages it afterwards.

Two recent books do. They come from two emerging disciplines, one for engineers and one for managers. Both are human-centred. Both are forward-looking.

## Human-Systems Integration: design it to be tangible

In *Human-Systems Integration: From Virtual to Tangible* (2020), Guy André Boy starts from a simple observation: we design virtually — models, simulations, prototypes — and forget that systems must become tangible, in two senses.

- **Physically tangible.** Does it hold up in the real world?
- **Figuratively tangible.** Does it make sense to the people who live with it?

The method is human-in-the-loop simulation from day one, with the future operators, until tangibility is reached. The direction is from rigid automation to flexible autonomy: technology, organisations and people designed together, so that the system can renegotiate who does what when the situation changes. It is the practical antidote to [Bainbridge's ironies]({{ '/series/05-new-perspectives-for-overcoming-ai-ironies/' | relative_url }}).

## Human-agent orchestration: manage it with a dial, not a default

In *The Human-Agent Orchestrator* (2026), Pascal Bornet, Jochen Wirtz, Thomas Stephens and colleagues argue, from four years of work with 432 organisations, that agents fail not for lack of capability but for lack of management infrastructure. Orchestrating hybrid human–AI teams is a new management discipline. Four of its tools:

- **The Orchestration Design Canvas** — six questions (Source, Success, Safety, Steering, Switch, Sharpen) that lay the foundation of a human-agent system, and a compass to steer it consciously once it runs.
- **The Autonomy Dial.** Autonomy is not a property of the agent. It is a management decision, set per task by capability and criticality. It is [Shneiderman's "high automation *and* high control"]({{ '/series/07-partnerships-under-human-centered-ai/' | relative_url }}) turned into an operating procedure.
- **The Supervision Trap.** Reviewing everything does not scale. Design the exceptions: when does the human take the controls back?
- **The Holiday Test.** If work piles up while you are away, you have a tool. If operations continue and only exceptions are flagged, you have a system.

## One sentence to tie them to this series

The book puts it plainly: *Agents do not take control. Humans abdicate it.* The [first post]({{ '/series/01-from-augmentation-to-cognitive-surrender/' | relative_url }}) called that cognitive surrender.

Boy and Bornet answer from two sides. Who is in command is first a design decision, then a management decision — never a default the model grants you.
